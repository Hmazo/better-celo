# Celo Camp Batch 5: The Accelerator Cohort That Made "Real-World Web3" Feel Concrete

In 2022, crypto had a credibility problem. The industry was still full of energy, builders, and capital, but the question around many projects was becoming sharper: what does this actually do for people?

Celo Camp Batch 5 is worth revisiting because it answered that question in a practical way. The cohort was not built around one narrow crypto trend. It brought together teams working on plastic recovery, ESG giving, identity, payments, gaming, creator tools, on-chain carbon markets, trade finance, anti-fraud infrastructure, and mobile-first financial access. In other words, Batch 5 looked less like a speculative showcase and more like a map of problems that blockchain infrastructure could make easier to coordinate, verify, fund, or distribute.

That made it a strong reflection of Celo's broader thesis: Web3 matters most when it reduces friction for real people and real organizations, especially in contexts where mobile access, low fees, and transparent settlement can change what is possible.

## What Was Celo Camp Batch 5?

Celo Camp is an eight-week virtual accelerator run by Upright in collaboration with the Celo Foundation. Its purpose is straightforward: help founders building on Celo turn early ideas into stronger products, businesses, and ecosystem relationships.

Batch 5 opened with real demand. According to the Celo Blog announcement, 30 teams were selected from 395 applicants across more than 75 countries. The selected projects covered several sectors, including regenerative finance, DeFi, creator economy products, NFTs, analytics, payments, and gaming.

The Celo Foundation's 2022 annual report later framed Batch 5 as part of a larger year of growth for Celo Camp. The report says Celo Camp selected 30 Batch 5 teams, 14 of which became finalists and presented to investors, mentors, and community members. It also lists total Batch 5 prize money at 55,100 cUSD and 23,000 CELO.

Those numbers matter, but the more important point is the shape of the cohort. Batch 5 was not trying to prove that crypto could create another trading venue. It was trying to prove that open financial rails could support climate projects, social impact platforms, creative work, cross-border workflows, and user-facing products.

## The Context: Builders Needed Use Cases, Not Hype

Batch 5 ran from May to July 2022, a period when the wider crypto market was under pressure. That context made the cohort more interesting. When markets are loud, almost anything can look like traction. When sentiment cools, the best projects have to explain who they serve, what problem they solve, and why a blockchain is useful at all.

Celo's answer has long been anchored in mobile access, stable assets, low fees, and regenerative finance. Nansen's Q2 2022 Celo report described Celo as an EVM-compatible chain launched with a focus on global payments and mobile users. The same report highlighted Celo's ReFi activity during that quarter, including carbon-market infrastructure and projects designed around social or environmental outcomes.

That is the lens through which Batch 5 makes the most sense. The strongest teams were not using Celo as branding. They were using it as infrastructure for specific coordination problems:

- How can recyclers prove that plastic was recovered?
- How can donors participate in social impact campaigns with clearer incentives?
- How can small businesses, creators, or communities receive payments without expensive rails?
- How can identity, KYC, or fraud prevention improve the safety of crypto products?
- How can climate and carbon assets become more transparent and liquid?

Those are not abstract protocol questions. They are product questions.

## The Winners Showed Two Versions of Impact

The two headline winners, Plastiks and eSolidar, showed different but complementary sides of the Batch 5 thesis.

### Plastiks: Turning Plastic Recovery Into a Verifiable Market

Plastiks won the Batch 5 Gold Prize. The Celo Foundation annual report describes Plastiks as a marketplace for utility NFTs that uses digital plastic recovery certificates to offset pollution. Celo Europe later noted that Plastiks earned 10,000 cUSD and 2,000 CELO for the Gold Prize.

The important thing about Plastiks is not that it used NFTs. It is that the NFT was attached to a real-world claim: plastic recovery. The platform connects companies with recovery projects and uses blockchain-based records to make that recovery easier to trace. Celo Europe describes Plastiks' model as one where companies sponsor plastic waste recovery around the world, with Plastic Recovery Guarantees improving traceability and creating a revenue path for projects doing the recovery work.

That is a practical ReFi loop:

- a company wants to support measurable environmental action;
- a recovery project needs funding;
- the buyer needs evidence;
- the network needs a transparent record.

This is where the "utility" part of utility NFTs starts to matter. In Plastiks' case, the token is not just a collectible. It is a public-facing proof mechanism tied to a material outcome.

Nansen's Q2 Celo report highlighted Plastiks' Hotel Lancaster Paris collection as a demonstration of this model. Hotel guests could buy NFTs connected to hotel experiences while supporting plastic recovery. The point was not only to sell a digital item; it was to attach a consumer experience to an environmental action and give the buyer a way to verify that connection.

### eSolidar: Extending Social Impact Tools With Web3

eSolidar, the Batch 5 Silver Prize winner, represented a different kind of builder. The team was not starting from a blank page. It already worked in philanthropy, ESG, corporate social responsibility, and charitable giving.

The Celo Foundation annual report describes eSolidar as a blockchain-based ESG platform. Nansen's report adds useful product context: during Celo Camp, eSolidar demonstrated an NFT sweepstakes prototype designed to support donation campaigns, including a pilot with Rock in Rio where donors could participate for NFT-linked experiences.

That is a useful distinction. eSolidar was not trying to replace the nonprofit sector with a token. It was asking how Web3 mechanics could make participation more engaging, transparent, and programmable for campaigns that already need trust.

For corporate giving teams, nonprofits, and cultural events, the problem is often not a lack of good intentions. It is measurement, engagement, reporting, and continued participation. A blockchain layer can help when it gives stakeholders a shared record of contributions and rewards without making the user experience harder.

This is the other side of Batch 5's impact story: Web3 can be valuable when it augments existing institutions, not only when it tries to disrupt them.

## The Cohort Was Broader Than ReFi

Plastiks and eSolidar were the most visible winners, but Batch 5's strength came from the spread of teams. The full selected list included projects such as:

| Theme | Example Batch 5 Teams | What They Pointed Toward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ReFi and climate | Plastiks, Flowcarbon, Climate Guardians, Athena Protocol | Environmental assets, recovery proofs, climate games, and measurement systems |
| Financial access | Alcancia, Gane, PiggyFi Africa, Maya Financial | Savings, mobile services, stablecoin access, and DeFi-based financial products |
| Creator economy | Birden, Flan, Kluest, NEFTME, KR8 | Publishing, freelance workflows, immersive content, social NFTs, and community markets |
| Trust and compliance | Authologic, Gueno, Yanda | KYC orchestration, fraud prevention, and DAO auditing |
| Payments and trade | Acc.Eth, Forward, SmartLoC | Event ticketing, foreign exchange, and order-to-payment workflows |

That mix is important. A healthy ecosystem needs more than one breakout category. It needs apps, infrastructure, compliance primitives, consumer experiments, and financial tools that can reinforce each other.

Batch 5 had that kind of diversity. It included projects close to end users, like gaming and creator tools, alongside more operational products, like KYC aggregation and smart letters of credit. It also included projects focused on emerging-market access, which fit Celo's emphasis on mobile-first adoption.

## Why Celo Was a Natural Fit

Celo Camp Batch 5 worked because the accelerator's themes matched the chain's design priorities.

First, Celo has consistently emphasized mobile usability. That matters for products aimed at users who may not have desktop-first habits or access to expensive financial infrastructure. A project that wants to reach freelancers, small merchants, donors, or emerging-market consumers cannot treat mobile as an afterthought.

Second, Celo's stable assets are useful for products where users need prices and payments that feel legible. Climate credits, donations, event experiences, remittances, and freelance work all become harder when the unit of account is too volatile or unfamiliar.

Third, low transaction costs matter for small actions. A donation, identity check, community reward, NFT claim, or mobile payment can fail as a user experience if the fee is too high relative to the transaction.

Finally, Celo's ReFi identity gave founders permission to build products that were not only financial in the narrow sense. Batch 5 teams could connect finance to environmental proof, social giving, local access, and public-good infrastructure without feeling outside the ecosystem's center of gravity.

## What Batch 5 Got Right

The most useful lesson from Batch 5 is that real-world Web3 products usually need three things at once.

They need a real stakeholder. Plastiks had companies and recovery projects. eSolidar had donors, events, nonprofits, and ESG teams. SmartLoC had trade partners. Authologic had apps that need local identity verification.

They need a clear reason for shared infrastructure. A blockchain should not be present just because it sounds modern. It should help with traceability, settlement, ownership, coordination, incentives, or interoperability.

They need distribution. This is where accelerators matter. A founder can build a clever protocol mechanic in isolation, but getting mentors, ecosystem partners, investors, and peer teams around the product can make the difference between a demo and an actual market.

Celo Camp's structure helped teams sharpen those three elements. The program gave founders time, visibility, mentorship, and a concrete demo-day deadline. That combination is especially valuable in Web3, where the distance between a technical prototype and a trusted user-facing product can be large.

## Why It Still Matters

Looking back from 2026, Batch 5 feels less like a time capsule and more like an early signal.

Many of the questions it explored are still central to blockchain adoption:

- Can on-chain records make climate claims more auditable?
- Can stablecoin rails make small payments and global transfers easier?
- Can social impact organizations use programmable incentives without confusing donors?
- Can consumer products hide crypto complexity while keeping crypto's benefits?
- Can emerging-market builders shape the products instead of only receiving them?

The industry has changed since 2022, but those questions have not gone away. If anything, they have become more practical. The most credible Web3 products now tend to look less like isolated token systems and more like software that makes real coordination cheaper, faster, or more trustworthy.

That is what made Celo Camp Batch 5 notable. It did not solve every challenge, and not every startup in any accelerator will become a durable company. But it gathered a set of founders who were building toward concrete outcomes: recovered plastic, better donation campaigns, mobile savings, safer onboarding, creator monetization, auditable DAOs, and cross-border workflows.

For a blockchain ecosystem, that is the right kind of ambition. It is not enough to ask users to care about decentralization as an ideology. The product has to make something they already need work better.

## A Practical Blueprint for Future Builders

For founders building on Celo, or any chain focused on real-world adoption, Batch 5 offers a simple blueprint:

1. Start with a problem that exists off-chain.
2. Identify the trust, payment, proof, ownership, or coordination gap.
3. Use the chain only where it improves that gap.
4. Design for mobile and low-friction usage from the beginning.
5. Measure impact in terms users understand, not only in crypto-native metrics.

That last point is especially important. Plastiks could talk about kilograms of plastic recovered. eSolidar could talk about donation campaigns and participant engagement. Financial inclusion projects could talk about savings, payments, access, and local currency needs. These are clearer than vague promises about disruption.

Celo Camp Batch 5 deserves attention because it showed how many forms "useful Web3" can take. It can look like a hotel NFT that funds plastic recovery. It can look like a sweepstakes tool for donors. It can look like KYC orchestration, a mobile savings account, a creator marketplace, a trade-payment workflow, or an anti-fraud product.

The common thread is not the token. The common thread is usefulness.

That is why Batch 5 still stands out: it treated blockchain as infrastructure for better coordination, and it gave founders a stage to prove that the next wave of crypto products could be judged by what they make possible in the real world.
